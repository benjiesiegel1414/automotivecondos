Buying a Car Condo: 9 Things to Check Before You Sign

November 6, 2026 · AutomotiveCondos.com

Most car condo regrets trace back to something knowable before closing: a door two feet too short, a declaration that bans the business you planned to run, an association with empty reserves, or a pre-construction timeline that slipped a year. None of it is hard to check — it just doesn't get checked, because buyers tour a gleaming showroom unit on a Saturday and sign on Monday. This guide expands the nine checks we recommend to every buyer into the questions, documents, and measurements behind each one. New to the category? Read What Is a Car Condo? first, and bring the 2026 cost guide so the numbers you're quoted have context.

1. Door Height and Width: Measure the Tallest Thing You'll Ever Own

The single most common and least fixable mistake. Overhead doors in garage communities typically come in 10x10, 12x12, and 14x14 foot configurations, and the opening is structural — you generally cannot enlarge it later.

  • A sedan or sports car clears anything. A lifted truck, Sprinter van, or boat on a trailer usually wants 10–12 feet. A Class A motorhome generally needs a 14-foot door — and that's before roof-mounted AC units, antennas, and satellite domes, which are exactly what hits the header.
  • Measure your actual vehicle at its true highest point, loaded as it travels, and add a safety margin. Then measure the door, not the brochure: as-built openings sometimes run a few inches shy of nominal.
  • Check width and the approach too: mirror-to-mirror width, the turning radius in the drive aisle, and apron depth. A 45-foot coach needs room to swing into the bay without a twelve-point turn.
  • Even if you don't own an RV, remember resale: taller doors widen your future buyer pool and hold a price premium.

2. Clear Ceiling Height and Lift Rules

Ceiling height determines whether the unit is storage or a real shop. Two separate questions:

  • Physical clearance. You want clear height — under the lowest sprinkler pipe, duct, or beam, not to the roof deck. A four-post storage lift with a car on top wants roughly 11–12 feet of clear height; stacking taller vehicles or running a two-post lift for service work can want more. Communities in our directory commonly offer 14–22 feet clear, which also determines whether a mezzanine is feasible.
  • Legal clearance. Some declarations restrict lift types, require engineer-stamped installs, cap mezzanine coverage, or require architectural approval for anything bolted to the slab. Confirm slab thickness and PSI rating if you're installing a two-post lift — the point loads are no joke.

Ask for the as-built drawings and the association's written lift policy, not a salesperson's verbal assurance.

3. The Declaration, Rules, and Zoning: What You're Actually Allowed to Do

The recorded declaration of condominium is the constitution of the community, and it binds you whether you read it or not. Before signing:

  • Permitted use. If you plan anything beyond vehicle storage — detailing side business, e-commerce inventory, photography, client visits, a man-cave with a kegerator — find the exact clause that allows or forbids it. Commercial activity, overnight stays, and subleasing are the three most commonly restricted uses.
  • Rental rules. Investors especially: can you lease your unit? Minimum terms? Association approval of tenants? Some communities ban rentals outright, which kills the investment thesis.
  • Zoning. The community sits in some municipal zoning category — often industrial or commercial — that independently limits use. The declaration can be more restrictive than zoning, never less. If your use matters, verify both layers.
  • Modification rules. Mezzanines, signage, exterior changes, flooring, electrical upgrades — know the approval process and fees.
  • Insurance-relevant prohibitions. Fuel storage limits, welding, spray painting, and hazardous materials rules protect everyone; make sure your hobby fits inside them.

Spend the few hundred dollars to have a real estate attorney read the declaration, especially for pre-construction purchases where documents can still change.

4. Power: Amps, Meters, and EV Reality

Electrical capacity is the most underestimated spec. Questions to pin down:

  • Panel size. 60A supports lights, openers, and a battery tender. A welder, compressor, lift, mini-split, and Level 2 EV charger running together wants 100–200A. Upgrading later may be possible but depends on the building's total service — ask what headroom exists.
  • Metering. Individually metered units are the standard you want; shared-meter arrangements billed through dues invite disputes.
  • EV charging. If you'll charge vehicles, confirm the declaration allows charger installs and the service supports them. Some older buildings physically can't add many 50A circuits.
  • 220V availability, outlet placement, and lighting circuits — cheap to spec now, annoying to retrofit.

5. Dues, Budget, and Reserves: Read the Association's Financials

Dues of $100–$400/month are typical across the category, but the number alone tells you nothing. What matters is whether the dues are honest:

  • Get the current budget. What do dues cover — master insurance, water, security, landscaping, management? A suspiciously low number usually means something (often reserves) is missing.
  • Get the reserve study. Roofs, pavement, gates, and fire systems all have replacement clocks. A community ten years in with thin reserves is a special assessment waiting to happen — a surprise bill of thousands per owner. Ask: when was the last reserve study, what percent funded are reserves, and have there been special assessments?
  • Pre-construction buyers: advertised dues are developer estimates and routinely rise after owner turnover when real costs surface. Ask how the developer subsidizes the budget during sellout and what the pro forma assumes.
  • Delinquencies. High owner delinquency rates stress budgets and can complicate financing.

6. Insurance: Two Policies, Not One

Understand exactly where the association's master policy stops and your responsibility starts — the declaration spells it out:

  • Master policy (funded by dues) typically covers the building shell and common elements. Confirm its coverage limits, deductible, and whether owners are responsible for a share of the deductible after a loss.
  • Your unit policy (HO-6-style or small commercial) covers interior build-out, betterments, contents, and liability. If you've put $60,000 of mezzanine, cabinets, and HVAC into the unit, insure it.
  • Vehicle coverage is separate again — collector-car and RV policies apply, and insurers often look favorably on gated, sprinklered, monitored facilities. Tell your agent exactly where the vehicles live.
  • Climate risk. In Florida and coastal markets, ask about wind/named-storm deductibles and flood zone status; in the north, ask about freeze protection. These shape both the master policy cost (your dues) and your own premiums.

7. Phasing and Pre-Construction: Buying a Promise

Early-phase pricing in successful communities has historically been the best deal in the category — and pre-construction is also where buyers take the most risk. Protect yourself:

  • Deposit safety. Is your deposit escrowed? Under what conditions is it refundable? What happens if the project doesn't reach its financing or pre-sale thresholds?
  • Timeline slippage. Assume delivery dates move. If you're selling other storage or a property to fund this, build slack into your plan, and check whether the contract gives you exit rights after a defined delay.
  • Spec drift. Get door sizes, ceiling heights, electrical service, and finish specs in the contract, not the brochure. Renderings are marketing.
  • Developer track record. Has this developer delivered garage communities before? Visit their previous projects and talk to owners there.
  • Turnover terms. When does association control transfer from developer to owners, and what condition must common elements and budgets be in at turnover? This is where underfunded reserves get discovered.

8. Community Culture: The Amenity Nobody Puts in the Spec Sheet

Two communities with identical buildings can be completely different places to own. One hosts cars-and-coffee every month, has a lively owners' group chat, and sells units by word of mouth; the other is a silent row of closed doors. Neither is wrong — but you should know which one you're buying into, because culture drives both your enjoyment and your resale market.

  • Tour on a Saturday morning, not a Tuesday afternoon. Count open doors.
  • Talk to two or three current owners without the salesperson present. Ask what they'd change.
  • Ask about events, the clubhouse calendar, and whether the association funds social activities or leaves it to owners.
  • Check the fit: a community of concours collectors and a community of contractors storing skid steers are both legitimate — but they're different neighbors, different noise profiles, and different rule enforcement vibes.

9. Resale Thinking: Buy the Unit You Can Sell

You will eventually be a seller. Buy accordingly:

  • Specs that resell: taller doors, more amps, climate control, bathrooms, end positions, and mezzanines consistently widen the buyer pool. The marginal cost at purchase is usually smaller than the premium at exit.
  • Market depth: a unit in a metro with several thriving communities and recorded comps (Florida being the extreme case — see the price data) is more liquid than the only garage community in a small market.
  • Rental fallback: if the declaration permits leasing, you have a plan B that supports value even when sales are slow.
  • Avoid over-personalization: a tasteful lounge sells; a themed build that only fits your hobby narrows the exit.

Common Mistakes We See Buyers Make

  • Buying the showroom unit's finish, not the community's fundamentals. Epoxy and LED strips are the cheapest part of the deal; reserves and declarations are the expensive part.
  • Trusting nominal door sizes. Measure the actual opening and the actual vehicle. Inches matter at 13 feet 6.
  • Skipping the reserve study. The most predictable five-figure surprise in condo ownership of any kind.
  • Assuming business use is fine. It frequently isn't, and enforcement is real once owner boards take over.
  • Buying too far from home. Owners 20 minutes away use their units constantly; owners an hour away visit quarterly and eventually sell. Check the map for options inside your real radius before settling.
  • Ignoring the exit. Paying up for the smallest, lowest-door, interior unit in a weak market is how you buy the one unit that won't move.
  • Underbudgeting the build-out. Shell buyers routinely spend $30–$100+ per square foot finishing; get bids before closing, not after.

Questions to Ask the Seller (or Developer)

  • What are the exact as-built door dimensions and clear ceiling height of this unit?
  • Can I have the declaration, rules, current budget, latest reserve study, and the last year of board meeting minutes?
  • Have there been any special assessments, and are any planned or discussed?
  • What does the master insurance policy cover, what is its deductible, and how is a loss allocated to owners?
  • What is the electrical service to this unit, and what upgrade capacity remains in the building?
  • Are lifts, mezzanines, and EV chargers permitted, and what is the approval process?
  • Can units be rented out? On what terms?
  • For new construction: is my deposit escrowed, what happens if delivery slips past a set date, and when does association control turn over to owners?
  • Why are you selling? (Resale sellers' answers are consistently informative.)
  • How many units have resold in the past two years, and at what prices? (Then verify against county records.)

A seller with nothing to hide answers all ten without flinching. Hesitation on the financial documents is itself an answer.
