How Much Does a Car Condo Cost in 2026?

November 6, 2026 · AutomotiveCondos.com

Car condo prices have moved fast. Five years ago, a deeded garage unit was a niche purchase that most real estate agents had never heard of; in 2026, it is a maturing asset class with its own comps, its own premium tiers, and — in hot markets — waitlists for pre-construction phases. This guide lays out what units actually cost nationwide right now, what drives the spread, what the ongoing ownership bill looks like, and how to run total-cost-of-ownership math before you commit. If you're still deciding whether the category fits you at all, start with What Is a Car Condo? and come back.

The National Picture in 2026

Across the 273 communities in our directory, most new and resale units trade in a band of roughly $175 to $325 per square foot for standard product — a shell or lightly finished unit in a gated, sprinklered community. Premium communities (full climate control, luxury clubhouses, marquee locations) run meaningfully higher, with top-tier units in South Florida, Scottsdale, and a handful of other markets commanding $400–$600+ per square foot once finished.

Translate that into whole-unit prices and the typical menu looks like this:

  • Entry units (600–900 sq ft): roughly $125,000–$275,000 depending on market and finish.
  • Mid-size units (1,000–1,600 sq ft): roughly $200,000–$475,000.
  • Large / RV-capable units (2,000–3,000+ sq ft): roughly $400,000 to well over $1 million in premium communities.

Those are wide ranges because this is a hyper-local product. The same 1,200 sq ft floor plan that sells for $230,000 in a Midwest metro can clear $500,000 in Tampa. Location, amenity level, and finish matter more than raw square footage.

What the Florida Data Shows

Florida is the deepest and most liquid car condo market in the country, which makes its recorded sales data the best window into the category's trajectory. The numbers are striking:

  • The median recorded sale for garage condo units statewide climbed from roughly $230,000 in 2021 to roughly $429,000 in 2026 — close to a doubling in five years, per county records.
  • In the Tampa Bay area, among the most active submarkets, the median recorded sale now sits around $750,000, reflecting a concentration of large, highly finished units in premium communities.
  • County assessors have noticed: assessed values on garage condo units rose about 11.5% for 2026, which flows directly into owners' property tax bills.

Two caveats keep this honest. First, medians shift with the mix — as developers deliver bigger, fancier product, the median rises even if like-for-like units appreciate more modestly. Second, Florida's run was supercharged by population inflows and a construction boom that other states haven't matched. Still, the direction is unambiguous: units in established communities have been appreciating assets, not depreciating toys. Browse the state's communities on our Florida page.

New Construction vs. Resale

Both routes have real tradeoffs:

Buying New / Pre-Construction

  • Pros: pick your unit location and door size, lock pricing before completion (early phases are routinely the cheapest a community will ever be), get a fresh building with modern fire protection and electrical, and spec the shell to your build-out plan.
  • Cons: you buy a rendering — delivery dates slip, finishes change, and dues are only estimates until the association has a real operating history. Developer-controlled associations can underfund reserves to keep advertised dues attractive. Deposit structures and escrow terms deserve a lawyer's read.

Buying Resale

  • Pros: what you see is what you get — a functioning association with a budget, reserves, rules, and a culture you can evaluate on a Saturday morning. Finished units often sell for less than the cost of replicating the build-out, because buyers pay for location and shell first.
  • Cons: limited inventory in hot markets, you inherit the previous owner's layout choices, and in appreciating markets you pay today's comps instead of yesterday's pre-construction pricing.

Our rule of thumb: in a market with active new development, tour both. Pre-construction wins on price per foot; resale wins on certainty. Either way, run the nine pre-purchase checks before signing.

What Drives the Price of a Specific Unit

Within a single community, unit pricing spreads for identifiable reasons:

  • Door height and width. A 14-foot door commands a premium over a 10-foot door on an otherwise identical unit, because it unlocks the RV and motorcoach buyer pool. This premium tends to hold at resale.
  • Finish level. A bare shell versus epoxy floors, insulation, mini-split HVAC, a mezzanine, a bathroom, and cabinetry can easily be a $50,000–$150,000+ difference. Build-out costs commonly run $30–$100+ per square foot depending on ambition.
  • Phase and timing. Early-phase buyers in successful communities have historically done best; final-phase pricing often runs materially above phase one in the same project.
  • Position. End units, units near the clubhouse, corner units with extra apron space, and units with drive-through potential all price above interior bays.
  • Power and plumbing. 200A service, EV circuits, floor drains, and private restrooms each add real value because retrofitting them later is expensive or impossible.
  • Climate control. In the Sun Belt, conditioned space is increasingly the dividing line between standard and premium pricing.

Ongoing Costs: The Monthly Bill After Closing

Purchase price is only chapter one. A realistic monthly budget for a typical mid-size unit includes:

  • Association dues: $100–$400/month typical. Basic drive-up communities sit at the low end; full-amenity, climate-controlled communities with clubhouses at the high end. Dues cover the master insurance policy, common-area maintenance, security, and reserves.
  • Property taxes: commonly $150–$600+/month equivalent. Roughly 1–2% of assessed value annually in most jurisdictions. Florida owners should note the ~11.5% assessed-value increase for 2026 flowing into bills.
  • Insurance (unit policy): roughly $30–$100/month. Your HO-6-style or commercial unit policy for interior build-out, contents, and liability — on top of the master policy your dues fund, and separate from vehicle policies.
  • Utilities: roughly $30–$200/month. Individually metered electric; climate control in a hot or cold climate is the swing factor.

Budgeting the Build-Out

Most new units deliver as shells: slab, walls, door, lights, a panel, and a sprinkler head. Turning a shell into the unit you pictured is a second budget, and it is where first-time buyers most often miss. Typical line items for a mid-size unit:

  • Epoxy or polished concrete floors: roughly $5–$12 per square foot depending on system and prep.
  • Insulation and wall finish: several thousand to $20,000+ depending on climate and how finished you want it to look.
  • Mini-split or packaged HVAC: commonly $5,000–$20,000 installed, scaling with volume — remember you're conditioning 16-plus feet of height, not 8.
  • Mezzanine: steel mezzanines typically run $15,000–$50,000+ installed depending on span and finish; stairs, railing, and permits included in the quote or they will surprise you.
  • Lift: $3,000–$8,000 for quality four-post storage lifts; more for two-post service lifts once you count electrical and slab verification.
  • Bathroom: if plumbing is stubbed, $8,000–$25,000; if it isn't, it may not be possible at any price — check before you buy.
  • Cabinets, lighting upgrades, audio, security: the long tail that turns $20,000 of essentials into a $75,000 project.

Rule of thumb: get real contractor bids before closing, and assume the finished-unit resale market will repay tasteful, broadly useful improvements (HVAC, mezzanine, bathroom) far better than taste-specific theming.

How to Read Comps in a Thin Market

Outside Florida, Texas, and Arizona, many markets have only one or two communities, and public comps are sparse. Three habits keep you from overpaying:

  • Pull county records yourself. Garage condo units are deeded parcels, so recorded sales are public. Search the community's address or the condo association's name in the county property appraiser or recorder database and build your own comp sheet — price, date, square footage, and $/sq ft for every resale in the project.
  • Separate shell comps from finished comps. A $410,000 sale with a $90,000 build-out inside it is not a comp for your shell purchase. Where you can, find out what was in the unit when it traded.
  • Watch days-on-market, not just price. In thin markets, asking prices are aspirations. A community where resales clear in weeks supports aggressive pricing; one where listings sit for a year does not, whatever the ask says.

Financing Notes

Car condos don't fit the conventional residential mortgage box, so financing is a patchwork: local and regional banks offering commercial or specialty condo loans (often 20–30% down, 15–25 year amortization), credit unions, developer-arranged lending programs on new projects, HELOCs against a primary residence, and plain cash — which remains the most common way these units trade. Rates typically run above residential mortgage rates. If you plan to finance, line up the lender before you shop; it narrows which communities and price points are realistic.

Total Cost of Ownership: Example Math

Take a concrete, middle-of-the-road scenario: a 1,200 sq ft unit at $275/sq ft = $330,000, bought with cash, plus a $45,000 build-out (epoxy, insulation, mini-split, mezzanine, cabinets).

  • Upfront: $330,000 purchase + ~$5,000 closing costs + $45,000 build-out = ~$380,000 in.
  • Annual carrying: dues at $250/mo ($3,000) + taxes at ~1.5% of a ~$300,000 assessed value ($4,500) + unit insurance ($700) + utilities ($1,200) = ~$9,400/year, or about $780/month.
  • Five-year cost if the unit's value were flat: ~$47,000 in carrying costs — call it roughly $780/month for 1,200 secure, powered, lift-ready square feet. Comparable rental space, where it exists at all, commonly runs $600–$1,200/month and builds zero equity.
  • Five-year cost with appreciation: if the unit merely tracks modest 3–4% annual appreciation, the value gain roughly offsets carrying costs. Florida's recorded five-year run (median ~$230K to ~$429K) obviously did far better than that — but treat that as history, not a promise. We don't project returns; we note that in established markets, owners have generally not been paying for a depreciating asset.

The honest framing: a car condo costs about as much per month to own as premium storage costs to rent, with the difference that you own the real estate at the end. Whether that trade wins depends on your market, your holding period, and how much you'll actually use the space — the owners happiest with the math are the ones at their unit every weekend.
