Ask this question in a car forum and you will get two answers shouted at equal volume: "best purchase I ever made" and "it's a toy, not an investment." Both sides are partly right, which is why this analysis is longer than a forum post. Garage condos have put up genuinely strong appreciation numbers in the hottest markets over the past five years — Florida's recorded sales data is striking — but they are also a niche asset with a thin buyer pool, real carry costs, and a construction pipeline that could blunt future gains. Below is the full bull case, the full bear case, the actual numbers, how professional buyers underwrite these units, how they compare to other alternative assets, and how you eventually get out. One note before the numbers: nothing here is financial, investment, tax, or legal advice. It is market analysis from a directory that tracks 273 communities across 42 states. Talk to your own advisors before buying anything.

What the Florida Numbers Actually Show

Florida is the deepest garage condo market in the country — roughly 100 of the communities in our directory are there — so it is the best place to look for real transaction data. Based on county recorded sales data, the median recorded garage condo sale in Florida went from approximately $230,000 in 2021 to approximately $429,000 in 2026 year-to-date. That is an increase of roughly 87 percent in five years, a period in which broad residential real estate rose far less.

Read this chart carefully rather than triumphantly. Three honest caveats. First, these are medians of recorded sales in a thin market, so the mix of what sold matters enormously — a year in which several large, highly built-out units close will drag the median up even if like-for-like prices are flat, and the dip from $375K in 2024 to $330K in 2025 followed by the jump to $429K in 2026 is at least partly a mix story. Second, newer communities are being built bigger and nicer, which raises medians without necessarily raising the value of an older unit you might buy. Third, 2026 is year-to-date, not a full year. Even with all of that said, the direction is unambiguous: prices have moved up substantially, and the trend is corroborated by county assessors — assessed values on Florida garage condos rose about 11.5 percent for 2026, and assessors are a conservative, lagging indicator. Something real is happening in this market; the open question is whether it continues.

The Full Bull Case

The case for garage condos as an appreciating asset rests on five legs.

  • Structural supply scarcity. Garage condos need cheap-ish industrial-adjacent land near wealthy populations, and that land is disappearing in exactly the metros where demand is strongest. Municipalities rarely champion garage projects over housing or retail, so entitlement is slow. New supply arrives in lumps and sells out.
  • A demand base that keeps growing. The collector car market has broadened well beyond retirees with Corvettes — enthusiast demand now spans everything from air-cooled Porsches to JDM imports to overlanding rigs — and every new collector needs climate-controlled space. Meanwhile, HOA-restricted suburbs keep pushing boats, RVs, trailers, and project cars out of driveways.
  • Utility beyond the garage. The "man cave" or clubhouse use case means buyers are paying for lifestyle, not just storage, which supports prices above pure warehouse economics. Many owners also run quiet businesses — detailing, small e-commerce inventory, a shop — where zoning allows.
  • Hard-asset characteristics. It is deeded real property with condominium title. You can insure it, leverage it, depreciate it when used in a business (ask your CPA), and pass it on. It is not a storage lease that reprices against you every year.
  • The track record, where it exists. The Florida data above is the headline, and anecdotally, early buyers in established Arizona and Texas communities report similar experiences: units bought from developers years ago reselling well above original pricing. Past performance guarantees nothing, but the pattern is consistent across the mature markets.

The Full Bear Case

Now the other side, stated just as plainly — because every one of these risks is real.

  • The buyer pool is narrow. When you sell a house, your buyer is "anyone who needs housing." When you sell a garage condo, your buyer is "someone within driving distance who has six figures of discretionary capital and a specific need for 1,000-plus square feet of enclosed space." In a strong economy that pool is deep enough; in a downturn, discretionary assets are the first thing nobody is shopping for. Expect longer marketing times than residential in any market, and much longer ones in bad markets.
  • Pipeline and oversupply risk. The same appreciation that excites you excites developers more. Our directory has grown steadily as new projects are announced, and in a few hot metros multiple communities are now under construction simultaneously. If several projects deliver into the same submarket at once, resale pricing on existing units will feel it. This is the single biggest medium-term risk in the strongest markets.
  • Carry costs are not trivial. You will pay association dues, property taxes (which rise with those assessed values), insurance, and utilities whether the unit earns anything or not. On a larger unit, annual carry can plausibly run into the mid four figures or beyond depending on the market and association. An asset that produces no income while costing real money every month only "works" financially if appreciation outruns the carry.
  • Custom build-outs don't fully transfer. The $150,000 of mezzanine, bar, bathroom, lifts, and epoxy you put into a unit is worth whatever the next buyer happens to value it at — often meaningfully less than it cost. Highly personalized units can be harder to sell, not easier. The appreciation data above reflects whole transactions, including build-outs that may have been done at yesterday's construction costs.
  • Thin data cuts both ways. The same low transaction volume that makes the medians jumpy makes it genuinely hard to know what any individual unit is worth. Appraisals are difficult, comps are scarce, and the bid-ask spread in slow periods can be wide.

Rental Yield: A Worked Example

Some buyers underwrite garage condos as income property. Here is an illustrative example — the assumptions are stated so you can swap in your own market's numbers, and real quotes in your metro will differ. Suppose you buy a 1,200-square-foot unit for $400,000 all-in. Comparable enclosed, climate-controlled space in your market rents for somewhere around $1.50 to $2.50 per square foot per month depending on metro and finish — call it $2.00, or $2,400 per month, $28,800 per year gross. Subtract association dues, taxes, insurance, and a vacancy allowance — plausibly $7,000 to $10,000 per year combined on a unit like this — and you are netting roughly $19,000 to $22,000, a net yield in the ballpark of 5 percent on cost before any financing. That is competitive with other small commercial real estate, but note what the number is sensitive to: one vacant year on a niche space with a narrow tenant pool crushes it, and some associations restrict or prohibit leasing entirely. Verify the association's rental rules before you underwrite a single dollar of income.

How Serious Investors Underwrite These Units

Buyers who treat this as an investment rather than a clubhouse look at a consistent short list:

  • Price per square foot versus replacement cost. The core sanity check: what does this community sell for per square foot, what did it sell for historically (county records are public), and what would it cost a developer to build the same thing today? Buying meaningfully below replacement cost is the classic margin of safety; buying far above it means you need the scarcity story to hold.
  • Association health. Read the budget and the reserve study. An association with thin reserves facing a future roof or paving bill will hit every owner with special assessments — and will scare off your future buyer. Well-run associations are a selling point; broke ones are a liability attached to your deed.
  • Door and shell specs. The physical characteristics that drive resale are boringly specific: door height (14-foot doors accommodate big rigs and lifts; 10-foot doors exclude them), clear ceiling height (16-plus feet enables mezzanines and stacking lifts), bay depth, power service (a 100-amp-plus panel matters to shop users and EV owners), HVAC capability, and drainage. Units with the best specs in a community command premiums and sell first; the awkward units sell last in every cycle.
  • Location within the location. End units, units near the clubhouse, and units with easy truck-and-trailer access carry premiums. Same logic as any real estate, one level down.
  • Exit assumptions. The honest underwriters assume a long marketing period on exit and still require the deal to work. If your model only works with a quick, top-of-market sale, you do not have a model, you have a hope.

Exit Strategies

Plan the exit before you enter. There are four realistic paths. Resale to an end user is the standard exit — maximize it by owning good specs in a healthy association and by listing where buyers actually look, including commercial platforms and directories like ours (owners and communities can list with us here). Resale inside the community is often faster: your neighbors and the association's waitlist are the warmest buyer pool in this asset class, which is another reason association health matters. Lease it and hold turns a stalled sale into an income bridge where rules allow, letting you wait out a soft market instead of hitting a weak bid. Estate or business transfer is the quiet fourth path — because it is deeded real property, it passes cleanly in ways a storage lease never could. What you should not count on is a quick, frictionless sale at full price in any market condition; that is the one exit this asset class does not reliably offer.

Verdict

Are garage condos a good investment? In the strongest markets, over the last five years, they demonstrably were — Florida's recorded sales medians rising from roughly $230K to roughly $429K is not a rounding error, and assessors raising values 11.5 percent for 2026 confirms the direction. Going forward, the honest answer is conditional: a well-specced unit, bought sensibly relative to replacement cost, in a healthy association, in a supply-constrained metro, held by someone who can comfortably carry it for years, has a credible path to doing well. A maxed-out unit bought at a record price in a metro with three competing projects under construction is a very different bet. Buy it first because you will use it; underwrite the investment case like a skeptic.
